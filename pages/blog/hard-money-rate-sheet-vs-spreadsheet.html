Hard Money Rate Sheet vs Spreadsheet: Why Private Lenders Outgrow Excel

Why private lenders outgrow Excel for Fix & Flip, bridge, and DSCR pricing — and what a real rate sheet engine actually replaces.

MW
Mike Williams
Founder, EZ Rate Sheet · September 1, 2026

Most private lending shops still price Fix & Flip, bridge, and DSCR deals in Excel. It works — until two loan officers quote different rates on the same file, a state overlay is missed, or the “master” sheet is three versions behind.

This is not an argument that spreadsheets are stupid. It is an argument that a rate sheet is a product: encoded loan logic, a branded term sheet, and a workflow so the quote and the file stay in the same place. Excel is a calculator. The gap shows up as margin leakage and as hours you do not invoice. See why private lenders leak margin.

What Excel still gets right

Spreadsheets are fast to stand up. A principal can model a one-off structure in twenty minutes. There is no vendor, no implementation call, and no seat license. For a shop doing a handful of deals a month with one person who “owns the sheet,” that can be enough.

The problem is not the first version. It is the tenth. Cells get overwritten. Named ranges rot. The construction product is a copy of the bridge product with three undocumented exceptions. Nobody wants to be the person who breaks the macros.

Where spreadsheets break in production

  • LTV/ARV bands, points, and state rules live in the head of whoever last edited the file
  • Term sheets are a separate Word or PDF template that does not inherit the quote
  • There is no deal home — status lives in email and a pipeline tab that is not the sheet
  • Two originators can produce two rates from the same borrower package

That last item is the expensive one. A 12–15 bps variance across the book is not a rounding error. It is a process failure. A rate sheet engine exists to make the quote repeatable.

If the only person who trusts the spreadsheet is the person who built it, you do not have a rate sheet. You have a private notebook.

When to leave Excel

Leave the spreadsheet when any of these are true:

  • More than one person quotes
  • You have more than one loan product with real overlays
  • Brokers are waiting on a term sheet while someone “updates the file”
  • You cannot reconstruct why last month’s loan was priced the way it was

If you are still one principal and a handful of files, Excel can wait. The day the sheet becomes tribal knowledge, you are paying the tech tax without even buying software.
