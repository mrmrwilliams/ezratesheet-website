**Subtitle:** A rate sheet is a promise. Most private lenders break it before the deal closes.
**Tags:** rate-sheet,private-lending,lender-operations,pull-through-rate,hard-money

**Copy:**

Your rate sheet isn't a document.
It's a promise — and most lenders break it before the deal closes.

I've helped build rate sheets for over 300 private lending shops. In almost every shop I walked into, some version of this was happening:

A borrower gets quoted 10.5% on a 12-month bridge. They go into underwriting. By the time the commitment letter lands, it's 10.875% — because the LO didn't know there was an add-on for properties over $1.5M in secondary markets. Nobody communicated that. Nobody flagged it. That's not a borrower problem. That's a rate sheet design problem.

Here's what breaks down specifically:

• Add-ons buried in footnotes that LOs don't read
• Tiered LTV adjustments that aren't clearly mapped to loan amounts
• Fee structures that differ between the verbal quote and the term sheet
• No version control — so LOs may be working off last quarter's pricing

The pull-through rate tells you everything. If a lender is closing 60% or less of what they commit on, their rate sheet is lying to their borrowers. Not intentionally — but systematically.

A clean rate sheet is engineered for the person quoting it, not just the person who built it. Every add-on should be findable in under 10 seconds. Every scenario should have one answer, not three interpretations.

## Post 07 — 2026-11-10 — Rate Sheet Pain

**Headline:** The rate sheet your LO is quoting from / *isn't the one* you think they're using.
**Subtitle:** Most rate sheet failures aren't pricing problems. They're version control, communication, and design problems.
**Tags:** rate-sheet,private-lending,lender-operations,pricing,hard-money

**Copy:**

The rate sheet your LO is quoting from isn't the one you think they're using.

I've been inside lending operations long enough to know how this happens. The rate sheet gets updated — maybe the floor rate changes, maybe a new product tier gets added, maybe there's a geographic restriction on rural properties. Someone sends it in an email. Somebody else saves it to their desktop. A third person is still referencing the version from six weeks ago that they bookmarked because it was easier to find.

Nobody is doing this intentionally. But the result is that three LOs in the same shop are quoting three different products to the same borrower profile.

This is the version control problem. And it's more expensive than most operators realize.

Here's what it costs:

Pull-through rate erosion. When your quotes don't survive contact with underwriting, borrowers lose confidence — and sometimes lose funding timing. You don't lose them on the current deal; you lose them on the next three. I've seen shops with pull-through rates under 55% trace the problem directly back to quote inconsistency — not underwriting standards, not property quality, not borrower profile. Just LOs working from different pricing documents.

Legal exposure. Verbal commitments and written terms that don't match are a disclosure problem. I've seen lenders face complaints and escalations over rate discrepancies that started with an outdated spreadsheet. In a few cases, those discrepancies showed up in loan applications in ways that created regulatory risk. It's a paperwork problem with a compliance tail.

Staff credibility. Your LOs look uninformed when they quote one number and the term sheet shows another. That's recoverable once. It becomes a pattern that's not. And when an LO loses credibility with a borrower, that's not just one relationship — it's every referral that borrower would have sent.

Competitive velocity. The lender who can quote accurately in under 10 minutes wins the deal that starts with 'I'm talking to a couple shops.' Shops with version control problems add 20-30 minutes to every non-standard scenario because someone has to verify which pricing is current before they can commit to anything.

So what does a well-designed rate sheet actually fix?

First: it has one home. Not a shared drive folder with twelve versions — one URL, one login, one current document. Everyone pulls from the same source.

Second: it's designed for the quoter, not the architect. That means every add-on is visible at the point of decision — not buried in footnotes. LTV tiers, property type adjustments, prepay scenarios — findable in under 30 seconds without calling the capital markets desk.

Third: it has a version history. Every change is dated and logged. When a borrower says 'I was quoted X two weeks ago,' you can pull the exact rate environment from that date and show your work. This matters both for internal accountability and for borrower disputes.

Fourth: it has a defined update process. Who approves rate changes? When do they take effect? How does the change get communicated to the team? These aren't complicated questions — but in most shops, nobody has written down the answers.

I've built over 300 of these systems. The lenders who have their rate sheet dialed in aren't just quoting faster — they're closing more of what they quote. That's the direct line between rate sheet design and pull-through rate. A 10-point improvement in pull-through on $10M/month origination volume is $1M in additional closed loans — from a documentation fix, not a market condition.

If you sent your rate sheet to a new LO today with zero context, how long would it take them to quote a scenario accurately?

The secondary market doesn't care how good your origination story is.

I want to be specific about what that means, because I've seen it play out too many times in both directions.

A lender has a strong product — well-underwritten fix-and-flip loans in growing metros, clean LTV distribution, conservative DSCR on their rental product. Their default rate is low. Their relationships with borrowers are strong. They have a compelling pitch.

And in a world where they're buying in volume — reviewing hundreds of loans per month — a lender whose tape requires manual reconstruction is a lender they deprioritize. They'll buy from whoever makes the diligence process easiest.

This is a documentation problem, not a lending problem. And it's almost entirely solvable before the loan is originated.

Here's the specific failure mode I see most often:

A lender closes a DSCR rental loan. The interest rate, points, and fee structure are captured in the commitment letter and signed by the borrower. But when the loan gets transferred to the servicer, the fee nomenclature changes. 'Origination fee' becomes 'lender fee' in the servicing system. 'Prepay penalty' is logged differently than the commitment. When an institutional buyer runs their diligence model against the tape, the numbers don't reconcile.

The lender isn't doing anything wrong. But the data tells an inconsistent story, and the buyer isn't equipped to manually reconcile 200 loans. They pass.

Here's what the lenders with strong secondary market access do differently:

They standardize data capture at origination. Every field that an institutional buyer uses in their template — borrower entity type, exit strategy, DSCR calculation methodology, property class — is captured in a consistent format at the point of application, not reconstructed at sale. Their intake form is mapped to the buyer template, not the loan officer's preference.

They build their underwriting criteria around buyer appetite. With 15+ active institutional buyers in the market — Toorak, Roc360, Genesis Capital, Kiavi, Verus, Anchor Loans, RCN, and others — there's enough public information about each buyer's preferences to map your product against their requirements before you originate. This isn't guesswork. It's product design.

They treat the rate sheet and the loan tape as connected. The pricing terms that show up in the commitment letter should flow directly into the loan record without manual re-entry. Discrepancies between what was quoted and what's in the servicing system are where secondary market transactions stall.

They audit their tape before approaching buyers. Not annually — quarterly. A clean tape review surfaces data integrity issues before they become a deal-killing surprise during due diligence. It also gives them a clear picture of their geographic and product concentrations — useful for knowing which buyer to approach first.

They don't wait until they have a large tape to access the secondary market. Small lenders with clean, well-documented loans get done at Roc360 and Genesis Capital. Size matters less than consistency.

## Post 18 — 2026-12-05 — Borrower Retention

**Headline:** Your borrower didn't leave for a *cheaper rate*. / They left because someone else was *easier*.
**Subtitle:** In a market where 63% of investors use multiple lenders, the lender they call first is the one who made it easiest to close.

**Copy:**

Your borrower didn't leave for a cheaper rate.
They left because someone else was easier.

I know this because when lenders lose a repeat borrower, the first assumption is price. 'We got undercut.' Maybe. But when I dig into what actually happened, it's almost never a margin problem. It's a friction problem.

Here's what 'easier' looks like in practice:

The competing lender confirmed receipt of the borrower's application within the hour. Not with a formal letter — with a text: 'Got it, we'll have a term sheet to you by tomorrow morning.' That's it. But the borrower knew someone was on it.

The competing lender's draw process had a defined turnaround. Three business days, acknowledged in writing. Not 'as soon as we can get to it.'

The competing lender remembered what the borrower did for a living and didn't ask them to re-explain their business model on the second deal. The intake form pre-populated from prior loan data.

None of that is a rate decision. All of it is a systems and communication decision.

Here's the compounding effect: 63% of active RE investors use multiple lenders simultaneously. That means your best borrower is always one bad experience away from routing the next deal somewhere else. And they often won't tell you why. The pipeline just goes quiet.

Retention is earned at every touchpoint — the quote turnaround, the processing communication, the draw timing, the follow-up after close. Each one is either building the case for coming back or eroding it.

The lenders I've worked with who have the strongest repeat borrower rates share one operating principle: every step of the borrower experience is defined, not improvised. The borrower always knows what to expect next.
