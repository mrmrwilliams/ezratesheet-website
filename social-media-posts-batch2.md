### Post 06 | Monday, June 22
**Type:** Long-form
**Pillar:** Secondary Market

---

**LinkedIn:**

The institutional secondary market for private lending paper has grown faster than most operators realize — and the requirements to participate have become clearer.

Here's what the conversation actually looks like when a private lender approaches an aggregator:

The buyer doesn't start with loan quality. They start with originator process. What does your pricing methodology look like? Are rates determined by a documented system or by the judgment of whoever's available? Can you show consistency across your tape — not just good loans, but loans that look like they came from the same place?

The reason is straightforward: aggregators buying pools of RTL, DSCR, or bridge loans are underwriting the originator, not just the loans. If your next 50 loans could look substantially different from your last 50 — because pricing lives in people's heads — that's a risk the buyer prices in. Usually in the form of a lower bid or a pass.

What most private lenders who could be selling paper lack isn't volume. It's documentation of process. The loans are often good. The story of how those loans were priced consistently is missing.

The fix isn't complicated. It's just building the operation with this in mind before you need it — because by the time you're ready to talk to a buyer, the tape already exists.

If secondary market access is something your operation is thinking about in the next 1–3 years, that conversation starts with how you're pricing deals today.

Something worth paying attention to in private lending right now: the gap between operators who are building systems and operators who are building volume.

We've worked with 300+ lending operations over the past several years. Here's the honest pattern we keep seeing in shops that have stalled at a ceiling they can't break through:

It's never the tools. It's always the architecture.

Every tool in the stack was a reasonable decision. The CRM was right for the team size at the time. The pricing spreadsheet worked when one person managed it. The doc platform came highly recommended. The pipeline tracker made sense when deals were simpler.

The problem isn't any one of these choices. It's that they were never made together. So now:

— Your rate quotes live somewhere different from your pipeline
— Your pipeline doesn't automatically update when a deal closes

The fix isn't "replace everything." Most of the time, it's something more targeted: identify the three highest-friction handoffs and eliminate the manual step in between. Start there.

The shops that have solved this aren't running on the most sophisticated software. They're running on the fewest necessary handoffs. That's the difference.

**LinkedIn:**

The anatomy of a loan that sells clean on the secondary market is simpler than most originators think.

It's not about exotic terms or premium collateral. It's about documentation and process consistency.

When we look at what actually creates friction in a sale — what causes a buyer to discount or pass — it almost always comes down to one of three things:

1. Pricing inconsistency: rates on similar deals vary enough that the buyer can't confidently underwrite the next tranche
2. Documentation gaps: servicing data, origination records, and property valuations that require reconstruction before the tape is usable
3. Process opacity: no clear record of how underwriting decisions were made, which makes the pool difficult to model

None of these are loan quality problems. They're origination and documentation problems. And they're fixable before you ever need to sell.

The shops that access the secondary market at the best terms aren't necessarily the largest. They're the ones whose operations tell a consistent, auditable story.

**LinkedIn:**

After working with 300+ lending operations, here's what we've come to understand about pricing systems — and why most shops get stuck.

The problem isn't that lenders don't care about pricing consistency. Most do. The problem is that the solution they've implemented — a spreadsheet, a shared document, a verbal understanding of the guidelines — wasn't designed for scale.

Here's what "built for scale" actually looks like in practice:

**Pricing is a system, not a document.** The rates update automatically when inputs change. Every LO pulls from the same source. No one is maintaining a spreadsheet that can drift from reality.

**Exceptions are logged, not improvised.** When a deal falls outside the standard guidelines, there's a process for handling it — not an informal conversation that may or may not get documented.

**The principal's time is in parameters, not approvals.** The operator reviews the pricing logic periodically. They're not in every deal because they don't need to be — the system handles the standard cases.

**The data is usable downstream.** When you need to report on your book, talk to a capital partner, or eventually consider secondary market options, the pricing history is there and auditable.

This isn't a technology pitch. It's a description of what operational maturity looks like in the shops that have stopped scaling by adding people and started scaling by building systems.

The rate sheet is where it starts. Everything downstream gets cleaner when that's right.
